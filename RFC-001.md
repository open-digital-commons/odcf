**A call to design citizen-owned, democratically governed public digital infrastructure for social communication**

## 0. How to read this document

This RFC is **a starting point, not a conclusion**. It is written to be argued with.

- Sections 1–3 describe the **problem** and propose **principles** that any solution should meet.
- Sections 4–6 lay out **precedents, the wider movement ODCF belongs to, and strategic options**, without choosing between them. Section 5 explains where ODCF fits among existing initiatives.
- Section 7 names the **hardest tension** in the whole proposal.
- Sections 8–9 contain the original **four pillars** and a **starting draft charter**. They are offered as raw material, not as decisions.
- Every open question is collected in [OPEN-QUESTIONS.md](OPEN-QUESTIONS.md).

Where this document sounds certain, treat that as an invitation to challenge it.

## 1. Summary

Social communication at global scale has become infrastructure: as essential to public life as roads, libraries or broadcasting. Yet it is almost entirely owned and operated by a small number of for-profit companies whose business model depends on extracting attention and personal data.

The Open Digital Commons Framework (ODCF) asks a simple question: **what would it take for social media to be owned by its users, work in their interest, and be governed democratically, with democratic states protecting it but never controlling it?**

ODCF is not the first to ask this. A European Citizens' Initiative, policy researchers, civil-society coalitions and infrastructure builders are already calling for, and building toward, public, open and independent social infrastructure (see §5). ODCF's contribution is narrower and, we hope, complementary: **an open, collaboratively drafted governance charter**, a concrete answer to how such a commons would be governed, funded and protected from capture, including capture by the states that fund it.

ODCF does not answer that question alone. It proposes a shared vocabulary, a set of principles, a starting draft, and an open process through which citizens, technologists, lawyers, researchers, existing initiatives and public institutions can answer it together.

## 2. The problem

1. **Ownership.** Users create the content, relationships and communities, but platforms own them. Leaving a platform usually means losing your audience, your history and your identity.
2. **Incentives.** Revenue from targeted advertising rewards engagement above wellbeing, accuracy or civic health. Outrage and compulsion are features, not bugs.
3. **Opacity.** Recommendation and moderation systems that shape public opinion are secret and unaccountable.
4. **Fragility of control.** A platform's rules, values and ownership can change overnight, following one owner's decision or one acquisition.
5. **Democratic deficit.** Decisions with enormous public consequences (what speech is allowed, what is amplified, who is banned) are made privately, without public participation or meaningful appeal.
6. **Concentration.** Network effects make it extremely hard for alternatives to grow, even when people want to leave.

Regulation, such as the EU's Digital Services Act, can limit some harms. But regulation alone does not change who **owns** the public square. ODCF asks whether a public alternative is possible.

## 3. Proposed principles

These are candidate principles. Any proposed solution, whether new, existing or hybrid, could be tested against them. **They are themselves open for revision.**

| # | Principle | In plain words |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | **User ownership** | Your content, identity and social graph belong to you and are portable. |
| P2 | **Non-commercial core** | The core infrastructure can never be sold, and never be funded by surveillance advertising. |
| P3 | **Openness** | Protocols, code, specifications and governance records are open. |
| P4 | **Algorithmic choice** | You can see, choose, change or switch off the systems that rank your feed. |
| P5 | **Democratic governance** | Ordinary citizens have a real, structural voice in decisions. |
| P6 | **Independence from capture** | No government, company or individual can buy, control or censor the infrastructure. |
| P7 | **Due process** | Moderation decisions are explainable and appealable. |
| P8 | **Interoperability** | Systems speak open standards, so that no one is locked in, including to ODCF itself. |
| P9 | **Sustainability** | Funding is stable, transparent, and structured to protect independence. |
| P10 | **Subsidiarity** | Decisions are made at the most local level that can handle them well (community, language, country) before the global level. |

## 4. Precedents worth learning from

No existing institution is a perfect model. Each offers lessons, including lessons about what goes wrong.

| Precedent | What it shows | What to watch for |
|---|---|---|
| **CERN** | Established in 1954 by treaty among European states. Member states fund it; scientists direct the science. The web was invented there and released to the public free of charge. | Works for research; untested for a mass public service with political stakes. |
| **Public service broadcasters** | Public money with legally protected editorial independence. | Recurring political pressure on budgets, boards and appointments, a direct warning for P6. |
| **Internet standards bodies** (IETF, W3C) | Open, multistakeholder standard-setting without an owner. | Participation is skewed toward large companies with the staff to attend. |
| **Wikipedia / Wikimedia** | A global commons built and governed largely by volunteers, funded by donations. | Volunteer governance is hard to scale and can become insular. |
| **Linux and open-source foundations** | Shared infrastructure that many competitors maintain together. | Foundations can drift toward the interests of corporate funders. |
| **Citizens' assemblies** (e.g. Ireland's) | Randomly selected citizens can deliberate well on hard, divisive questions. | Their recommendations are often advisory; their real power must be designed in. |

Contributors are invited to add precedents, especially from outside Europe and North America.

## 5. The wider movement, and where ODCF fits

ODCF does not start from zero, and it should not try to stand apart. A growing movement is working toward public, open and independent social infrastructure. As of October 2026, it includes at least the following strands.

### 5.1 Calls for public action
- **European Citizens' Initiative "European Public Social Network."** Registered by the European Commission on 4 March 2026. It asks the Commission to propose legislation creating a public social media platform at European level: publicly funded, publicly overseen, impartial and independent of political pressure. If it gathers one million signatures across at least seven member states, the Commission must formally respond.
- **Civil-society coalitions.** For example, a German alliance including Wikimedia Deutschland, the Free Software Foundation Europe, Mastodon gGmbH and digital-rights groups, launched in November 2025. It asks governments for sustained public funding of the Fediverse (proposing €30 million a year), for public institutions to use open networks alongside commercial ones, and for a multilingual European public media platform on open protocols.

### 5.2 Policy thinking
- **Open Future** and others have argued that public money should not build a single state platform, but a **"digital public space"**: interoperable open protocols, public-interest feed curation modelled on public service media, and publicly funded safety and moderation infrastructure shared across networks. This is close to Option C in §6.

### 5.3 Open protocols and infrastructure
- **The Fediverse**: thousands of independently run servers (Mastodon and many others) that interoperate through **ActivityPub**, a W3C standard.
- **The AT Protocol**: the open protocol behind Bluesky, built around portable identity and user-chosen feeds.
- **Independent AT Protocol infrastructure**, such as **Free Our Feeds** (a public-interest campaign launched January 2025) and **Eurosky** (European-hosted identity and data infrastructure, launched April 2026). By their own descriptions, these still depend partly on Bluesky's core services, especially for moderation, and are working toward greater independence.
- **Matrix**: an open standard for decentralised real-time communication, stewarded by a non-profit foundation.
- **Public funding programmes** such as the EU's Next Generation Internet (NGI) initiative, which has funded many open internet projects.

### 5.4 What is still missing, and ODCF's role
Across these efforts there is broad agreement on the **goal**: public, open, independent social infrastructure. What remains largely undeveloped is a worked-out answer to the **governance** question:

- Who would govern a public digital commons, and how would citizens take part?
- How would several states fund it without any of them controlling it?
- How would it resist capture by governments, companies or individuals?
- What happens when member states' laws conflict, or when the institution fails?

ODCF exists to draft that answer **in the open**, as a resource for the efforts above. It is not a rival campaign. In practice this means:

- **supporting** the European Citizens' Initiative and similar calls, by offering a concrete model of what an independent public network could look like;
- **inviting critique** from the people already building open infrastructure, who know far more about its realities than any charter can;
- **staying protocol-neutral** unless and until contributors decide otherwise (§6);
- **looking beyond the EU**, so that the model can also work for Canada and other democracies.

> Contributors: this landscape is moving quickly. Please help keep this section accurate, and tell us about initiatives we have missed, especially outside Europe.

## 6. Strategic options (undecided)

The most basic question is deliberately left open:

**Option A: Build new.** Design a new protocol and platform from first principles.
- *For:* Full freedom to embed the principles from the start.
- *Against:* Very expensive and slow; repeats work already done; must overcome network effects alone.

**Option B: Adopt and govern existing.** Choose one or more existing open protocols and provide what they lack: funding, legal protection, governance, public-interest services.
- *For:* Fast; builds on working technology and existing communities; respects others' work.
- *Against:* Inherits design decisions and governance arrangements ODCF did not choose; requires partnership with existing communities rather than direction of them.

**Option C: A protocol-neutral commons layer.** ODCF defines **principles, certification and governance** and funds public-interest services (identity, moderation, discovery, archiving) that work across several open protocols.
- *For:* Avoids picking winners; strengthens the whole open ecosystem. Close to the "digital public space" approach proposed in current policy debate (§5.2).
- *Against:* Weaker coherence; more complex to explain and govern.

Note that the governance questions in §7 and the charter in §9 arise under **every** option. However public money is spent, someone must decide how it is governed and protected. That is why ODCF can make progress on governance while the strategic question remains open.

There may be other options. Proposals are welcome as new RFCs (see [CONTRIBUTING.md](CONTRIBUTING.md)).

## 7. The central tension: support without control

ODCF asks democratic states to **fund and legally protect** this infrastructure, while also guaranteeing that **no government can control or censor it**. This is the hardest problem in the proposal, and the first one critics will raise. It deserves careful design, not reassurance.

Candidate safeguards, all open for debate:

- **Multi-state funding by treaty or long-term agreement**, so no single government's budget decision can starve the system.
- **Multi-year funding commitments** set out in advance, not renegotiated annually.
- **Funding by formula**, for example a fixed share of a digital services tax, rather than discretionary grants.
- **Strict separation of roles**: funders verify that money is spent properly; they have **no** vote on content, moderation or algorithms.
- **Distributed, multi-jurisdiction infrastructure**, so no single state can switch it off.
- **Open protocols and data portability**, so that if the institution is ever captured, users and communities can leave and rebuild elsewhere (exit as the last safeguard).
- **Transparency by default**: published budgets, decisions, government requests and moderation statistics.

Contributors are especially invited to study where public broadcasters and other independent public bodies have lost their independence, and why.

## 8. The four pillars

The original framing grouped the work into four pillars, each corresponding to a Working Group.

### Pillar 1: Legal and diplomatic structure → [WG 1](working-groups/WG1-policy-law.md)
How can an international non-profit be structured (for example as an international non-profit association, a foundation, a federation, or a treaty organisation) so that no single government or corporation can ever buy, control or censor it?

### Pillar 2: Technical and data architecture → [WG 2](working-groups/WG2-protocols-architecture.md)
How can the data/protocol layer be strictly separated from the application/interface layer, so that users own their identity, content and social graph through open standards (such as ActivityPub, AT Protocol, Decentralized Identifiers)?

### Pillar 3: Citizen governance and safety → [WG 3](working-groups/WG3-deliberation-safety.md)
How can sortition (randomly selected citizen juries), layered moderation and open algorithm choice replace opaque corporate algorithms and centralised moderation, while protecting people from harassment and harm?

### Pillar 4: Funding and anti-capture → [WG 4](working-groups/WG4-economics-funding.md)
How can public funds (research programmes, innovation grants, digital services tax revenue) pay for public digital space without introducing political interference?

## 9. Starting draft charter (raw material)

The following outline is the original seed text. It assumes a single consortium (provisionally called the **Public Digital Commons Consortium, PDCC**). That assumption may not survive the discussion in Section 6.

### Article I: Core mandate
1. The PDCC is an international, non-profit digital commons established to maintain public-interest social media protocols and applications.
2. The infrastructure shall never be sold, commercialised for targeted advertising, or transferred to private corporate ownership.

### Article II: Governance triad
Governance is divided among three independent bodies:
1. **Citizen Assembly**: direct and sortition-based civic oversight.
2. **Expert and Civil Society Council**: human rights, privacy and technical auditing.
3. **Funder and Institutional Oversight Committee**: public funding compliance; **zero editorial control**.

### Article III: User rights and algorithmic choice
1. Every user retains full cryptographic ownership of their content and social graph.
2. Users have an explicit right to choose, modify or disable recommendation algorithms via an open API.

### Articles not yet written (contributions invited)
- **Article IV:** Selection, size, term and rotation of the Citizen Assembly
- **Article V:** Powers of each body and resolution of disputes between them
- **Article VI:** Content moderation standards, transparency and appeals
- **Article VII:** Data protection, jurisdiction and responses to government requests
- **Article VIII:** Funding rules and safeguards against capture
- **Article IX:** Membership and withdrawal of states and institutions
- **Article X:** Amendment procedure (including which articles, if any, can never be amended)
- **Article XI:** Dissolution: what happens to the commons, the data and the code if the consortium fails
